Mental Health: Where to Find Psychological Support

War, flight, separation from loved ones and a new language leave marks. Feeling exhausted, anxious or sad is a normal reaction to an abnormal situation. In Germany, psychological help is part of health care, and asking for it is nothing to be ashamed of.

Updated: 28 October 20265 min readZusammenLeben

Common reactions after war and flight

Many people who fled Ukraine experience sleep problems, nightmares, constant worry about relatives, irritability, difficulty concentrating, headaches or a feeling of emptiness. Some feel guilty for being safe while others are not. Children may become clingy, withdrawn, aggressive or start wetting the bed again. These reactions are common and often become weaker with time, safety and routine. When they last for months or make daily life very hard, professional support helps.

When to seek help

  • You can barely sleep or eat for weeks.
  • You avoid people, places or news because of memories.
  • You feel hopeless or cannot manage work, the course or the children.
  • You use alcohol, sleeping pills or other substances to cope.
  • You have thoughts of not wanting to live. In this case, seek help immediately.

In an acute crisis

If you or someone close to you is in danger, call 112. In Bavaria, the crisis services (Krisendienste Bayern) can be reached around the clock on 0800 655 3000, free of charge. They advise by phone and can send a team in urgent situations. The telephone counselling service (TelefonSeelsorge) answers 24 hours a day at 0800 111 0 111 and 0800 111 0 222, anonymously and free. More numbers are on our emergency page.

Start with your family doctor

Your family doctor is a good first contact. You can talk openly about stress, sleep and anxiety; doctors are bound by confidentiality. The doctor can check physical causes, prescribe medication if needed, issue a sick note and refer you to specialists. Some symptoms such as exhaustion or heart palpitations have both physical and emotional causes, so this first visit is useful in any case.

Psychotherapy covered by health insurance

Psychotherapy is covered by statutory health insurance. You do not need a referral for the first appointment. The path usually looks like this:

  1. Book an initial consultation (psychotherapeutische Sprechstunde) with a licensed psychotherapist, directly or through the appointment service 116 117.
  2. In this conversation the therapist assesses what you need: short support, a longer therapy or another service.
  3. If therapy is recommended, there are trial sessions, and the therapist applies to the health insurance fund.
  4. Therapy then takes place regularly, often once a week.

Waiting times for therapy can be long. Put yourself on several waiting lists and use other support in the meantime.

Language in therapy

Therapy works best in a language you can express feelings in. Some therapists in Bavaria speak Russian or Ukrainian; the appointment service and psychotherapist search portals let you filter by language. If no therapist speaks your language, ask about working with an interpreter. Online and video therapy with licensed therapists is also possible in some cases.

Psychosocial centres and counselling services

Psychosocial centres for refugees and torture survivors offer specialised help, often with interpreters. In our region, social psychiatric services (Sozialpsychiatrischer Dienst) of the districts and welfare organisations offer free counselling for adults in crises, without health insurance formalities. Migration counselling services know local offers and can refer you.

Tip: you do not need to explain everything at once. It is enough to say "I am not well and need someone to talk to". Professionals will guide the conversation.

Support for children and teenagers

Children often show stress through behaviour rather than words. Talk to the class teacher and the school social worker; they see your child every day and can help. Family and educational counselling centres (Erziehungsberatungsstellen) advise parents free of charge. For children and teenagers there are special child and adolescent psychotherapists, also covered by insurance. Young people up to 25 can reach the free online counselling service krisenchat by chat, around the clock.

Medication

For some people, medication helps alongside talking, for example for severe sleep problems, depression or anxiety. Medication is prescribed by a doctor after an examination and is covered by insurance, with the usual small co-payment for adults. Do not take sleeping pills or tranquillisers from friends or from abroad without a doctor's advice, as some cause dependence. If you already take medication from Ukraine, bring the packaging to your doctor so it can be continued safely.

Peer support and self-help groups

Talking to people who have lived through something similar can be very relieving. Self-help groups (Selbsthilfegruppen) meet in many towns, some especially for Ukrainian women, mothers or older people. Regional self-help contact points can tell you which groups exist nearby. Community meetings, church groups and our own gatherings are also places where you are not alone with your thoughts.

What helps in everyday life

  • A daily routine: fixed times for sleep, meals, the course, walks.
  • Movement: walking, sports clubs, swimming. Physical activity reduces stress.
  • Contact: talk to people you trust, join a language café or a community group.
  • News breaks: limit the time spent reading war news, especially before sleep.
  • Small goals: one step at a time, such as one appointment or one form a day.

Grief and separated families

Many families live apart: one partner in Ukraine, grandparents far away, or relatives lost in the war. Grief can come in waves, sometimes long after the event. Grief counselling groups exist in many towns, often through churches or hospices, and some are open to people of any faith. Allow yourself and your children to grieve; rituals such as lighting a candle or keeping photos can help.

Mental health and your rights

Seeking psychological help does not affect your residence permit. Information from therapy is confidential. If you are unable to work for a time, your doctor can certify it, and the Jobcenter must take it into account, for example by postponing a course. Tell the Jobcenter if appointments or obligations are too much for you right now.

Helping someone else

If a friend or family member seems to be struggling, listen without judging, offer practical help and encourage them to see a doctor or call a counselling line. If they speak about ending their life, take it seriously, stay with them if possible and call 112 or the crisis service.

How we help

We listen, help you find a doctor, therapist or counselling centre, and accompany you to first appointments when possible. Our volunteers speak Ukrainian and Russian. Ask for help.

Useful official sources

  • Germany4Ukraine: medical care and psychological support
  • 116117: appointment service and on-call doctors (English)
  • krisenchat: free chat counselling for young people up to 25

General information, not legal advice. Rules change; always check the official sources linked above.

Frequently asked questions

Is psychotherapy free?

Yes. Psychotherapy by licensed therapists is covered by statutory health insurance.

Do I need a referral?

No. You can book an initial consultation directly or through the appointment service 116 117.

Who can I call at night?

The crisis services of Bavaria on 0800 655 3000 or TelefonSeelsorge on 0800 111 0 111. In danger, call 112.

Does therapy affect my residence permit?

No. Seeking psychological help has no effect on your residence status and is confidential.

Is there help for children?

Yes. School social workers, family counselling centres and child and adolescent psychotherapists.

Need personal help?

Our volunteers answer in Ukrainian, Russian, German and English. Free of charge.
