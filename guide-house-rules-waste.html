House Rules, Waste Sorting and Good Neighbours

Living together in a German apartment building follows rules that are often not explained to newcomers. Most conflicts with neighbours or landlords come from small misunderstandings. Knowing the rules makes daily life calmer and protects your tenancy.

Updated: 28 October 20265 min readZusammenLeben

The house rules (Hausordnung)

Most rental buildings have written house rules (Hausordnung), often attached to the lease or posted in the stairwell. They regulate quiet hours, cleaning duties, use of shared rooms, rubbish, parking and pets. They are part of your rental contract, so breaking them repeatedly can lead to warnings from the landlord. Ask for a copy if you did not receive one, and have it translated if needed.

Quiet hours

Quiet hours (Ruhezeiten) usually apply at night, typically from 10 pm to 6 or 7 am, and in many buildings also at lunchtime and all day on Sundays and public holidays. During these times, avoid loud music, drilling, vacuuming, slamming doors and loud conversations on the balcony or in the stairwell. Children's noise from normal play is generally accepted, but jumping and running late in the evening often causes complaints.

Waste separation

Germany separates rubbish carefully, and each town has its own system. In Upper Franconia the common bins are:

  • Residual waste (Restmüll, often grey or black bin): what cannot be recycled, such as hygiene articles, vacuum bags, ashes.
  • Organic waste (Biomüll, brown bin, where available): food scraps, coffee grounds, garden waste.
  • Paper (Papier, blue bin or container): newspapers, cardboard, packaging made of paper.
  • Packaging (yellow bag or yellow bin, Gelber Sack): plastic, metal and composite packaging such as drink cartons.
  • Glass: public containers sorted by colour: white, brown, green. Throw glass only during the day, not on Sundays.

Your town publishes a waste calendar (Abfallkalender) with collection days, often also as an app. Put bins out in the evening before or early on the collection day.

Tip: many drink bottles and cans carry a deposit (Pfand). Return them to supermarket machines and get money back instead of throwing them away.

Bulky waste and hazardous waste

Old furniture, mattresses and large items are collected as bulky waste (Sperrmüll), usually by appointment with the district waste service. Electronic devices, batteries, paint and chemicals go to recycling centres (Wertstoffhof) or special collections. Never leave items on the street without an appointment; this can lead to a fine.

Airing and preventing mould

German apartments are well insulated, which keeps heat in but also moisture. Air several times a day by opening the windows fully for five to ten minutes (Stoßlüften), especially after cooking, showering and in the morning. Keeping windows tilted all day wastes heating and does not remove moisture well. Do not dry large amounts of laundry in closed rooms without airing. If mould appears, inform the landlord in writing quickly.

Cleaning duties

In many buildings tenants take turns to clean the stairwell, entrance and sometimes the pavement, including clearing snow in winter (Kehrwoche or Winterdienst). A sign or plan shows whose turn it is. If you cannot do it, arrange a swap with a neighbour. In other buildings a cleaning company does it, and the cost is part of the service charges.

Shared rooms

Cellars, laundry rooms, attics and bicycle rooms are shared. Keep stairwells and corridors free; shoes, prams and furniture there can block escape routes and are often forbidden. Use the washing machine room only at the times stated in the house rules.

Balconies, barbecues and smoking

Barbecues on balconies are allowed in some buildings, restricted or forbidden in others; check the house rules and use electric grills where smoke bothers neighbours. Smoking on the balcony is generally allowed, but be considerate. Satellite dishes and other installations on the façade usually need the landlord's consent.

Pets

Small animals like fish or hamsters are generally allowed. Dogs and cats often require the landlord's consent; check your lease before getting one. Dogs must be registered with the town and require a dog tax (Hundesteuer), and liability insurance is recommended.

Getting along with neighbours

A short introduction when you move in, with a greeting and your name on the doorbell and letterbox, helps a lot. If you plan a party, let neighbours know in advance. If a neighbour complains, listen and try to find a solution together. Germans often address problems directly, which can feel harsh but is usually not meant personally. Small gestures, such as a greeting in the stairwell, build trust.

Repairs and damage

If something breaks, such as the heating, a window or a tap, inform the landlord or property management in writing with photos. Do not hire workers yourself without agreement, because you may have to pay. Small repairs may be the tenant's responsibility up to a limit if the lease says so. If you cause damage, your personal liability insurance usually pays.

Service charges statement

Once a year you receive the statement of service charges (Nebenkostenabrechnung), which lists costs for heating, water, waste, cleaning and caretaker. Check it and send it to the Jobcenter if you receive benefits. If you do not understand it, tenants' associations and we can help.

Keys, doorbell and post

Lost keys can be expensive in Germany, especially in buildings with a central locking system, where the whole system may have to be replaced. Personal liability insurance with key cover helps. Put your full name on the doorbell and letterbox, as many official letters are delivered only if the name is visible. If you live with others, add every surname.

Moving out: notice, handover and deposit

Tenants can usually terminate a lease with three months' notice, in writing and signed. When you move out, return the apartment in the condition the lease requires; many leases ask for small cosmetic repairs only in certain cases. At the handover, a record is signed and meter readings noted. The landlord may keep the deposit for some months to settle the final service charges, but must then return it with interest, minus justified claims. If the Jobcenter paid the deposit as a loan, inform it about the move.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Putting the yellow bag out on the wrong day or with wrong contents, so it is not collected.
  • Drying laundry indoors in winter without airing, which leads to mould.
  • Leaving prams or shoes in the stairwell.
  • Ignoring the cleaning plan or snow clearing duty.
  • Making noise after 10 pm or on Sundays.

Frequently asked questions

When are quiet hours?

Usually from 10 pm to 6 or 7 am, often also at lunchtime and all day on Sundays and holidays.

What goes in the yellow bag?

Packaging made of plastic, metal and composite materials, such as drink cartons. Not paper or glass.

How do I dispose of old furniture?

As bulky waste, usually by appointment with the district waste service.

How do I prevent mould?

Air several times a day with windows fully open for a few minutes, especially after cooking and showering.

Can I have a dog?

Often only with the landlord's consent. Dogs must be registered and require a dog tax.

Our volunteers answer in Ukrainian, Russian, German and English. Free of charge.
