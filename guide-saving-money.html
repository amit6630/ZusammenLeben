Saving Money in Everyday Life

Life in Germany is expensive, especially when you start with little. With some local knowledge, a family can save a lot every month without going without the essentials. These tips come from the experience of families we have supported in Upper Franconia.

Updated: 28 October 20265 min readZusammenLeben

Plan a monthly budget

Write down what comes in each month (benefits, wages, child benefit) and what must go out (rent if you pay it yourself, electricity, phone, transport, insurance). What is left is for food, clothes and everything else. Many banking apps show spending by category. A simple notebook works too. Seeing where money goes is the first step to saving.

Shopping for food

  • Discount supermarkets such as Aldi, Lidl, Netto, Penny and Norma are much cheaper than many full-range supermarkets, often with similar quality.
  • Own brands (Eigenmarken) are usually cheaper than famous brands.
  • Weekly leaflets (Prospekte) and supermarket apps show offers; plan meals around them.
  • Reduced items shortly before their best-before date are often marked with red or yellow stickers.
  • Markets at the end of the day sometimes sell fruit and vegetables cheaper.

Cooking at home, buying basic ingredients and planning a weekly shopping list save more than any single offer.

Food banks (Tafel)

The Tafel food banks collect surplus food from shops and pass it on to people with low income for a small symbolic contribution. There are Tafel outlets in many towns in Upper Franconia. You usually register once with proof of income, such as a Jobcenter decision, and then receive a card for a fixed day of the week. The offer varies, but often includes fresh vegetables, bread and dairy products.

Tip: using the Tafel is normal and nothing to be ashamed of. Many German families with low income use it too.

Clothes and household items

Second-hand clothes stores run by welfare organisations (Kleiderkammer, Sozialkaufhaus) sell clothes, toys and household goods very cheaply, sometimes for free for people with benefits. Flea markets (Flohmarkt), children's bazaars (Kinderbasar) in spring and autumn, and online platforms such as Kleinanzeigen offer used items; the category "free" (zu verschenken) contains furniture and toys people give away.

Free and cheap leisure

  • Libraries lend books, films, games and often language course materials; children's cards are usually free or very cheap.
  • Parks, playgrounds, hiking trails and lakes in the Fichtelgebirge and Frankenwald cost nothing.
  • Museums and swimming pools often offer discounts for families with benefits; ask with your decision.
  • The education and participation package pays a monthly amount for children's clubs, sports or music lessons.
  • Community events, church groups and our own gatherings are free.

Phone, internet and subscriptions

Compare phone tariffs; prepaid or no-frills providers are often much cheaper than big brands. Check your bank statement for subscriptions you no longer use, such as streaming services, apps or gym contracts, and cancel them. The Deutschland-Ticket is cheaper than single tickets for regular travellers.

Energy and water

Switch off devices instead of leaving them on standby, use LED bulbs, wash at lower temperatures and with full loads, and use lids when cooking. Heat rooms moderately and air briefly with windows fully open. These habits reduce the yearly electricity and heating bill and avoid back payments.

Medicines and health

Ask the pharmacy for cheaper equivalent medicines (Generika) when buying without prescription. Prescription medicines cost only a small co-payment, and children are exempt. If co-payments add up during the year, you can apply to your health insurance fund for an exemption after reaching your personal limit.

Insurance: what you need and what not

Personal liability insurance is strongly recommended and inexpensive. Many other insurance products sold at the door or by phone, such as expensive accident or legal insurances, are not necessary for most families. Before signing, ask the consumer advice centre.

Avoiding debt traps

  • Avoid "buy now, pay later" offers and instalment purchases for phones and electronics.
  • Do not use the overdraft facility (Dispokredit) for everyday spending; the interest is high.
  • Be careful with contracts signed at the door or by phone; you can usually cancel within 14 days.
  • Never ignore reminders (Mahnungen); costs grow quickly.

If you are already in debt, free debt counselling from welfare organisations helps you make a plan. A protected account (P-Konto) keeps a basic amount safe from seizure.

Benefits you may be missing

Check that you receive everything you are entitled to: child benefit, the education package for school and Kita, the broadcasting fee exemption, and once you work, the child supplement and housing benefit. Many families lose hundreds of euros a year simply because an application was never made. Our guide to family benefits explains them.

Buying used furniture and electronics

For furniture, used is often the best value; check social department stores and online platforms first. For electronics such as washing machines or fridges, refurbished devices with a warranty can be a good compromise. Before a big purchase, ask the Jobcenter whether it is covered by the initial furnishing allowance.

School supplies and children's clothes

Children grow fast, so buying everything new is expensive. Children's bazaars, swap groups in Messenger chats and hand-me-downs from other families cover a large part. For school supplies, the education package pays a lump sum twice a year; buy basic items at discounters at the start of the school year, when they have special offers. Many schools also keep second-hand sports clothes or musical instruments to lend.

Cooking together and sharing

Families who cook larger portions and share with neighbours or friends, or who buy bulk products together, save money and build friendships at the same time. Community gardens in some towns let you grow vegetables cheaply. Ukrainian community groups often organise exchange markets for clothes and household goods.

Where to get advice on money

Consumer advice centres give independent advice on contracts, energy, insurance and debts, partly free or for a small fee. Debt counselling services of welfare organisations are free. If you are unsure whether a contract or offer is a good idea, ask before signing, not after.

Saving for your children

Even small regular amounts add up. A savings account for your child, or putting aside part of the child benefit when you work, creates a reserve for school trips, a bicycle or the driving licence later.

How we help

We share local tips, collect donated furniture and children's items through our network, help with Tafel registration and connect families with debt counselling. Ask for help.

Frequently asked questions

What is the Tafel?

A food bank that passes on surplus food to people with low income for a small symbolic contribution.

How do I register at the Tafel?

Once, with proof of low income such as a Jobcenter decision. You then receive a card for a fixed day.

Where can I get cheap clothes?

In second-hand stores of welfare organisations, at children's bazaars, flea markets and online platforms.

Are there discounts for families with benefits?

Often yes, for museums, swimming pools and leisure. Ask and show your benefit decision.

What if I am already in debt?

Contact a free debt counselling service and consider a protected account (P-Konto).

Need personal help?

Our volunteers answer in Ukrainian, Russian, German and English. Free of charge.
