Finding Your First Job in Germany

Work brings income, language practice and new contacts. In Upper Franconia many employers in industry, logistics, care and gastronomy are looking for staff. This guide shows how to start.

Updated: 26 October 20265 min readZusammenLeben

Why a first job matters

Work brings income, but also German practice, new contacts and a daily structure. In Upper Franconia many employers in industry, logistics, care, cleaning, gastronomy and retail are looking for staff, and many are open to people who are still learning German. A first job does not have to be your final career. It is a step that makes the next steps easier.

Where to look

  • Jobcenter and employment agency: your caseworker and the job placement service (Arbeitsvermittlung) know local vacancies and can fund training or a trial period.
  • Job portal of the Federal Employment Agency (Jobbörse), plus portals such as Indeed and StepStone. Filter by town and save searches.
  • Temporary work agencies (Zeitarbeit), which often hire quickly in industry and logistics. Contracts are regular employment with the agency.
  • Local companies directly: many firms in Hof, Selb, Münchberg and Helmbrechts welcome a short visit with a CV, especially smaller businesses.
  • Your network: neighbours, the school, the church, volunteers and the Ukrainian community. Many first jobs are found through people, not ads.

Types of employment

  • Minijob: a small job with a low monthly income limit and simplified rules. Good for a start, but it gives little social insurance.
  • Part-time and full-time employment (sozialversicherungspflichtig): includes health, pension, unemployment and long-term care insurance. Even part-time regular work is usually better for your future residence options.
  • Vocational training (Ausbildung): two to three and a half years in a company and at vocational school, with a monthly allowance. Open to adults too, and often the fastest route to a recognised German qualification.
  • Self-employment: allowed with a §24 permit. You register a trade (Gewerbeanmeldung) at the town hall and inform the tax office. Get advice first, for example from the chamber of commerce.

Before you sign

  • Ask for a written contract and read it with someone you trust before signing.
  • Check the hourly wage: the legal minimum wage applies to you too, and some sectors have higher collective wages.
  • Check working hours, place of work, probation period (Probezeit), holidays and notice period.
  • You need your tax ID (Steuer-ID), which arrives by post after you register your address, your social insurance number and your bank details.
  • Never pay money to get a job, and never work without a contract. Undeclared work has no insurance and can cause problems with benefits and residence.
Report to the Jobcenter: tell the Jobcenter about any job before you start and send every pay slip. Part of your income is not counted, so working almost always leaves you with more money in total.

Reading your pay slip

Your monthly pay slip (Lohnabrechnung) shows gross pay (Brutto), deductions for income tax and social insurance, and the net amount paid to your account (Netto). Your tax class (Steuerklasse) depends on family status and affects how much tax is withheld. Keep all pay slips: the Jobcenter needs them, and so does the tax return. Filing a tax return (Steuererklärung) through the free ELSTER portal often brings money back.

A German CV in one page

German employers expect a short, clear CV (Lebenslauf) in table form, with a photo optional, contact details, your language levels and your experience in reverse order. Add recognised or translated diplomas and any German course certificates. For office jobs, a short cover letter (Anschreiben) explains why you want this job. For many practical jobs, a CV and a phone call are enough. Our volunteers can help you prepare both.

The interview

Be on time, bring copies of your documents and residence permit, and prepare a short introduction in German: who you are, what you did in Ukraine, what you can do now. It is normal to say that your German is still improving and that you attend a course. Employers value reliability and willingness to learn. Ask about working hours, training on the job and when you can start.

Your rights at work

  • Sick leave: inform your employer on the first day. From a certain day, usually the fourth, you need a doctor's certificate. You keep your wage for up to six weeks.
  • Holidays: at least 20 working days a year on a five-day week, often more by contract.
  • Working time: as a rule, no more than eight hours a day on average, with breaks.
  • Equal treatment: discrimination because of origin, language or religion is prohibited.

If you have problems with an employer, free advice is available from counselling services for migrant workers and from trade unions.

German at work

Many workplaces in our region are used to colleagues who are still learning German. Learn the key words of your job first: tools, safety instructions, shift times. Ask colleagues to speak slowly and do not be afraid of mistakes. Job-related language courses can be combined with work, sometimes partly during working hours, and the Jobcenter or employment agency can fund them. Employers often support this, because better German means safer and more independent work.

Getting to work

In rural Upper Franconia, getting to work is often the real challenge. Check bus and train connections before you accept a job with early or late shifts. The Deutschland-Ticket covers local and regional transport in all of Germany for a monthly price, and many employers subsidise it as a job ticket. Ask colleagues about car-sharing. The Jobcenter can sometimes cover travel costs at the start of a job, or support a driving licence if it is needed for work.

Childcare and work

For parents, work often depends on a Kita place or school care. Register early and tell potential employers which hours you can work. Many employers in our region offer shifts that fit school times. Ask the Jobcenter whether childcare costs can be covered while you start work.

Frequently asked questions

Am I allowed to work in Germany?

Yes. With a §24 permit you may work as an employee or self-employed from the first day.

Do I lose my benefits if I work?

Your benefits are reduced, but part of your income is not counted. Working almost always leaves you with more money in total.

Does the minimum wage apply to me?

Yes. The legal minimum wage applies to all employees in Germany.

Can adults start vocational training?

Yes. Ausbildung is open to adults and often leads fastest to a recognised German qualification.

Should I file a tax return?

It is often worth it. With the free ELSTER portal many employees get money back.
