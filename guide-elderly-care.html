Older People and Long-Term Care in Germany

Many Ukrainian families came to Germany with grandparents or older parents. Older people have their own rights and support in Germany, from basic income in old age to help at home when daily life becomes difficult. This guide explains the system.

Updated: 28 October 20265 min readZusammenLeben

Income for older people

People who have reached retirement age, or who can permanently no longer work, generally do not receive Jobcenter benefits. Instead, the social welfare office (Sozialamt) of the district pays basic income support in old age (Grundsicherung im Alter) if income and savings are not enough. It covers living costs, rent and heating within limits, and health insurance. Apply at the social welfare office of your district; staff can explain which documents they need.

Ukrainian pensions

If an older family member receives a Ukrainian pension, it usually counts as income and reduces German support. Report it to the social welfare office. Whether and how a Ukrainian pension can be paid abroad depends on Ukrainian rules; ask the Ukrainian pension fund or the consulate. Keep records of all payments.

Health care for older people

Older people with Jobcenter or social welfare benefits are insured in statutory health insurance or receive equivalent coverage. A family doctor coordinates care, prescribes medicines and refers to specialists. Glasses, hearing aids and walking aids can be prescribed; for adults, glasses are usually only partly covered. Bring all medical documents and medication packages from Ukraine to the first appointment.

When daily life becomes difficult: care levels

If someone needs help with washing, dressing, eating, moving or organising daily life for a longer period, they can apply for long-term care benefits. The application goes to the care fund (Pflegekasse), which is part of the health insurance fund. An assessor from the medical service (Medizinischer Dienst) visits at home and evaluates independence in daily life. The result is a care level (Pflegegrad) from 1 to 5.

Tip: before the assessor's visit, write down for a few days what help the person needs, how often and for how long. Be present at the visit and describe a normal day honestly, including bad days.

What care insurance pays

  • Care allowance (Pflegegeld): money for the person, if relatives or friends provide care at home.
  • Home care services (ambulante Pflege): professional carers visit for washing, medication, dressing.
  • Relief amount (Entlastungsbetrag): a monthly amount for household help or support services.
  • Day care (Tagespflege): the person spends the day in a facility and returns home in the evening.
  • Short-term and respite care: when relatives are ill, on holiday or need a break.
  • Care aids and home adaptations: such as a care bed, a walk-in shower or grab rails, partly paid.

The amounts depend on the care level. Benefits can often be combined.

Free care counselling

Everyone who applies for care benefits has a right to free care counselling. Care support centres and counsellors of the care funds explain options, help with applications and organise services. Ask your health insurance fund for the counselling point responsible for your district.

Care homes

When care at home is no longer possible, a care home (Pflegeheim) can be an option. Care insurance pays a fixed share; the rest, including accommodation and food, is paid by the resident. If income is not enough, the social welfare office can pay the remaining costs as help with care (Hilfe zur Pflege). Visit several homes and ask about waiting lists, which can be long.

Relatives who care

Family members who provide care at home can get pension contributions paid by the care fund under certain conditions, free care courses, and leave from work for a short or longer period. Caring is exhausting; use day care, respite care and the relief amount to take breaks. Self-help groups for caring relatives exist in many towns.

Power of attorney and advance directive

A power of attorney (Vorsorgevollmacht) allows a trusted person to act for someone who can no longer decide, for example in medical, financial or official matters. An advance directive (Patientenverfügung) records which medical treatment a person wants or refuses at the end of life. Without these documents, a court may have to appoint a legal guardian. Templates are available from the Federal Ministry of Justice and counselling services; a notary is not always required.

Safety at home

Falls are a major risk in old age. Remove loose rugs, use night lights, install grab rails in the bathroom and keep frequently used items within reach. A home emergency call system (Hausnotruf), a button worn on the wrist or neck, connects to a service centre around the clock; the care fund pays part of it for people with a care level. Some services work with Russian- or Ukrainian-speaking staff; ask when you book.

Medication management

Many older people take several medicines. Ask the family doctor for a medication plan (Medikationsplan), a printed list of all medicines with doses, and keep it with the health card. Pharmacies can check for interactions. Weekly pill organisers help avoid mistakes, and home care services can manage medication if a care level is granted.

If the application is refused

If the care level seems too low or the application is refused, you can file an objection within one month. Ask for a copy of the assessor's report and check whether all difficulties were recorded. Care counselling centres and social associations help with objections free of charge.

Social life for older people

Loneliness is a big issue for older people who left their home country. Senior clubs, parish meetings, walking groups and our own community gatherings offer company. Some welfare organisations run visiting services. Simple German courses for seniors exist in some towns, often slower and more relaxed than the integration course.

Severe disability pass

Older people with chronic illness or limitations may qualify for a severe disability pass (Schwerbehindertenausweis), which brings discounts, tax relief and, in some cases, free local transport. See our guide on disability.

End of life

Palliative care and hospice services support seriously ill people and their families, at home or in a hospice, free of charge through health insurance. Hospice associations also accompany grieving relatives. Talk to the family doctor early if a loved one is seriously ill.

How we help

We help families apply for basic income support and care levels, prepare for the assessor's visit, find day care or home care services, and connect older people with community groups. Ask for help.

Frequently asked questions

Who pays income support for pensioners?

The social welfare office of the district pays basic income support in old age, not the Jobcenter.

How do I apply for a care level?

At the care fund of your health insurance. An assessor visits at home and decides the care level.

Can relatives be paid for caring?

The person in need receives a care allowance, which can be passed on to the caring relatives.

What is a power of attorney?

A document allowing a trusted person to decide and act for someone who can no longer do so.

Who pays for a care home?

Care insurance pays a share; the rest is paid by the resident or, if needed, by the social welfare office.

Need personal help?

Our volunteers answer in Ukrainian, Russian, German and English. Free of charge.
