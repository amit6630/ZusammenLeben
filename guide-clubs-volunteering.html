Clubs and Volunteering: Finding Your Place

In German towns and villages, social life happens in clubs. Sports, music, the volunteer fire brigade, gardening or a choir: joining a club is the fastest way to meet neighbours, practise German and feel at home. Volunteering works the same way, and it is open to you too.

Updated: 28 October 20265 min readZusammenLeben

Why clubs matter in Germany

Germany has hundreds of thousands of registered clubs (Vereine). In small towns like those in Upper Franconia, the sports club, the music society and the fire brigade are the centre of village life. They organise festivals, train children, and bring together people of all ages. For newcomers, a club offers something a language course cannot: regular, relaxed contact with local people who share an interest.

Sports clubs

Almost every town has a multi-sport club, often called TSV, SV or FC, offering football, gymnastics, athletics, swimming, volleyball, table tennis, martial arts and fitness. Membership fees are moderate and lower for children and families. Many clubs let you try a few training sessions for free before joining. Ask at the training ground or on the club's website.

Paying for children's clubs

If your family receives Jobcenter benefits, housing benefit or the child supplement, the education and participation package pays a monthly amount for your children's club membership, music lessons or similar activities. Apply with the club's membership confirmation; the money is often paid directly to the club. This means many children can join at no cost to the family.

Music, choirs and culture

Music schools offer instrument lessons for children and adults, sometimes with reduced fees. Brass bands (Blaskapellen), choirs and church choirs welcome new members, often without auditions. Libraries, adult education centres and cultural associations run reading groups, painting courses and theatre. Singing in a choir is an excellent way to practise pronunciation.

The volunteer fire brigade

In most German villages and small towns, the fire brigade (Freiwillige Feuerwehr) is run by volunteers. Adults can join after a basic training, and children and teenagers can join the youth fire brigade (Jugendfeuerwehr). Members train regularly, help in emergencies and are highly respected in the community. Ask the fire brigade in your town about joining; good German is helpful but not required at the start.

Other ways to help: Red Cross, THW and more

Organisations such as the German Red Cross (DRK), the Federal Agency for Technical Relief (THW), the Johanniter or Malteser train volunteers in first aid, disaster relief and social services. They welcome people with medical, technical or language skills. Your knowledge of Ukrainian and Russian can be very valuable in social work, interpreting for newcomers or helping older people.

Volunteering for newcomers

Many Ukrainians already volunteer: translating for other families, helping at the Tafel, supporting language cafés, organising children's activities. Volunteering (Ehrenamt) is allowed with your residence permit. It gives purpose, structure and references for later job applications. Expense allowances for volunteers are tax-free up to a certain amount, and the Jobcenter does not count a part of them as income; report them anyway.

Tip: volunteering is a great reference. When you apply for jobs later, a letter from the organisation confirming your engagement shows reliability and German skills.

The Bavarian volunteer card

People who volunteer regularly in Bavaria can apply for the Bavarian volunteer card (Ehrenamtskarte) through their district. It gives discounts at swimming pools, museums and some shops, and it is a sign of recognition for your engagement. Ask the volunteer coordination office of your district.

Church and faith communities

Catholic and Protestant parishes in Upper Franconia run family groups, youth groups, senior meetings and charity shops, and many are open to people of every faith. Ukrainian Orthodox and Greek Catholic communities hold services in several Bavarian cities, sometimes in local churches; ask in the Ukrainian community. Faith communities are often a place of practical help as well as spiritual support.

Ukrainian community groups

Ukrainian associations and informal groups organise celebrations of national holidays, Ukrainian Saturday schools, folk dance, embroidery workshops and children's activities. They keep culture and language alive for your children. Many also cooperate with German clubs, which is a good bridge between both communities.

How to find clubs near you

  • The website of your town or district usually has a list of local clubs (Vereinsverzeichnis).
  • Notice boards in town halls, supermarkets and schools advertise events and training times.
  • Local newspapers and community bulletins (Gemeindeblatt) report on club activities.
  • Ask your children's school, Kita or your neighbours.

Your first visit

Do not worry about your German. Go to a training session or meeting, introduce yourself briefly, and say that you are new and want to try. Most clubs are happy about new members, especially young families. Bring a friend if it helps. After a few weeks, you will know people in town who greet you on the street.

Festivals and traditions

Clubs are behind most local festivals: the village fair (Kirchweih or Kerwa in Franconia), Christmas markets, summer festivals and sports tournaments. Visiting these events is free or cheap and a relaxed way to meet people. Bringing a Ukrainian dish to a club party or offering to show Ukrainian traditions is usually very welcome and opens conversations.

Activities for older people

Many towns have senior clubs, walking groups, card-playing afternoons and coffee meetings in parishes. These are good places for grandparents who came from Ukraine and often feel isolated. Some welfare organisations offer visiting services where volunteers regularly visit older people at home.

Teenagers and young adults

Youth centres (Jugendzentrum), youth groups of clubs and churches, and sports teams help teenagers make friends outside school. Youth migration services (Jugendmigrationsdienste) advise young people up to 27 and run group activities. For teenagers who feel lost between two languages, a team or a band can make the difference.

Costs and insurance

Club fees are usually paid once a year by direct debit. Members are insured during club activities through the club's insurance. Some clubs ask members to help at festivals a few hours a year; this is part of club life and a good chance to get to know people.

How we help

We know many clubs in Upper Franconia, help with membership forms and the education package application, and connect families with volunteers who accompany them on their first visit. Ask for help.

Frequently asked questions

Can I volunteer with a §24 permit?

Yes. Volunteering is allowed. Report expense allowances to the Jobcenter if you receive benefits.

Who pays my child's sports club?

The education and participation package pays a monthly amount if your family receives benefits.

Do I need good German to join a club?

No. Clubs are a great place to learn German. Most welcome new members warmly.

Can adults join the volunteer fire brigade?

Yes, after basic training. Children and teenagers can join the youth fire brigade.

How do I find clubs near me?

Check the town website, notice boards, the local newspaper, or ask the school and neighbours.

Our volunteers answer in Ukrainian, Russian, German and English. Free of charge.
