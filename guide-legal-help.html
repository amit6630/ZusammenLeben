Getting Legal Help in Germany

Letters from authorities, a landlord who keeps the deposit, an employer who does not pay: legal problems are stressful, especially in a foreign language. In Germany, people with low income can get legal advice for little or no money, and many free counselling services help before a lawyer is needed.

Updated: 28 October 20265 min readZusammenLeben

Do not ignore official letters

The most important rule: open every letter and react in time. Many German letters contain a deadline (Frist), and if you miss it, a decision can become final even if it was wrong. A notice from an authority usually includes instructions on legal remedies (Rechtsbehelfsbelehrung) at the end, which say how and by when you can object. If you do not understand a letter, bring it to a counselling service immediately, not after the deadline.

Common deadlines: objection (Widerspruch) against a notice from the Jobcenter or another authority: usually one month. Objection to a court payment order (Mahnbescheid): two weeks. Action against dismissal at the labour court: three weeks after receiving the dismissal.

Free counselling before a lawyer

Many questions can be solved without a lawyer. These services are free or very cheap:

  • Migration counselling for adults (MBE) and youth migration services: help with residence, authorities and letters, run by Caritas, Diakonie, AWO, the Red Cross and others.
  • Consumer advice centres (Verbraucherzentrale): contracts, mobile phone and internet, energy bills, debt collectors, online shopping. Advice costs a small fee or is free in some cases.
  • Tenant associations (Mieterverein): for members, advice and support in disputes with landlords, often with legal expenses insurance included.
  • Faire Integration: free advice for refugees and migrants on labour law, wages, working hours and dismissal, also in Ukrainian and Russian.
  • Debt counselling (Schuldnerberatung): free at welfare organisations; see our guide to saving money.

Legal aid certificate (Beratungshilfe)

If you have little income and need a lawyer's advice outside court, you can apply for legal aid (Beratungshilfe) at the local court (Amtsgericht) of your place of residence. You bring proof of income, for example a Jobcenter notice, and the papers about your problem. If approved, you receive a certificate (Berechtigungsschein) and choose a lawyer. The lawyer may charge only a small personal contribution of 15 euros, which can also be waived. You can also go to a lawyer first and apply afterwards, but then the risk is yours if the application is refused.

Court cost aid (Prozesskostenhilfe)

For court proceedings, people with low income can receive court cost aid (Prozesskostenhilfe). The court checks your income and whether your case has a reasonable chance of success. If granted, the state pays your court fees and your own lawyer, fully or in instalments. If you lose, you may still have to pay the other side's lawyer in civil cases, so ask about this risk before you sue.

Courts where you do not need a lawyer

At the social court (Sozialgericht), which decides about Jobcenter benefits, health insurance, pensions and disability, proceedings are free for insured people and benefit recipients, and you can sue without a lawyer. At the labour court (Arbeitsgericht), you can also act yourself in the first instance, and each side pays its own lawyer there regardless of the result. At the local court, small civil cases are possible without a lawyer.

Residence law questions

Questions about temporary protection, changing residence status or family reunification are complex. Migration counselling services give first orientation. For difficult cases, such as a refused extension or a criminal record that may affect residence, a lawyer specialising in migration law (Fachanwalt für Migrationsrecht) is recommended. Our guide to residence permits explains the basics.

Problems at work

Unpaid wages, missing employment contracts, too many hours or sudden dismissal are common problems for newcomers. Keep records: write down your working hours, keep wage slips and messages from your employer. You have the right to the minimum wage and paid holiday even in a mini-job. Trade unions advise and represent their members, and Faire Integration helps everyone for free. Read more in our guide to your first job.

Problems with the landlord

Typical disputes concern the deposit, utility bills (Nebenkostenabrechnung), repairs, mould and rent increases. Report defects in writing and keep a copy. Check the annual utility bill carefully: you can ask to see the receipts. The landlord must return the deposit after you move out, usually within several months, minus justified claims. A tenant association membership pays off quickly in such cases.

Contracts and consumer traps

Door-to-door sellers, phone calls with sudden contracts, expensive subscriptions and fake invoices target newcomers too. For contracts concluded at the door, by phone or online you usually have a 14-day right of withdrawal (Widerrufsrecht). Do not sign anything you do not understand, and never pay an invoice from an unknown company before checking it. If a debt collector writes, check whether the claim is real; the consumer advice centre helps.

If the police contact you

If you receive a summons as an accused person, you are not obliged to make a statement to the police, and you have the right to consult a lawyer first. You have the right to an interpreter. As a witness, you must usually appear when summoned by the public prosecutor or court. If you are a victim of a crime, you can report it at any police station; victim support organisations such as Weisser Ring help free of charge.

Interpreters and translations

In criminal proceedings, the court provides an interpreter free of charge. In other proceedings and at authorities, the rules differ; many counselling services work with volunteer interpreters. For official documents such as certificates, you often need a sworn translator (beeidigter Übersetzer); see our guide to documents and translations.

Finding the right lawyer

Lawyers in Germany often specialise. The title Fachanwalt shows proven expertise in an area such as labour, tenancy, family, social or migration law. The German Lawyers' Association runs a search service where you can filter by area and language. Ask about costs at the first contact: a first consultation has a legally capped fee for consumers.

How we help

We help you understand letters, note deadlines, find the right free counselling service, apply for legal aid at the local court and prepare documents for a lawyer. We do not give legal advice ourselves, but we make sure you reach someone who does. Ask for help.

Frequently asked questions

What is Beratungshilfe?

Legal aid for advice outside court for people with low income. You apply at the local court and pay a lawyer at most 15 euros.

How long can I object to a Jobcenter notice?

Usually one month after receipt. The deadline is stated at the end of the notice.

Do I need a lawyer at the social court?

No. Proceedings at the social court are free for insured people, and you can act without a lawyer.

Where do I get free advice on work problems?

Faire Integration advises refugees free of charge, also in Ukrainian and Russian; trade unions help members.

Can I cancel a contract signed at the door?

Usually yes, within 14 days, for contracts concluded at the door, by phone or online.

Need personal help?

Our volunteers answer in Ukrainian, Russian, German and English. Free of charge.
