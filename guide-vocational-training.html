Vocational Training (Ausbildung) Explained

In Germany, most skilled workers do not go to university: they complete vocational training. It combines work in a company with lessons at a vocational school and is paid from the first month. For young people and adults from Ukraine it is one of the most reliable routes to a good job.

Updated: 28 October 20266 min readZusammenLeben

What an Ausbildung is

An Ausbildung is a state-recognised vocational qualification. Most trainings follow the dual system: three to four days a week you work and learn in a company, one or two days you attend vocational school (Berufsschule), sometimes in blocks of several weeks. Training usually takes two to three and a half years and ends with an exam at a chamber, such as the chamber of industry and commerce (IHK) or the chamber of crafts (HWK). The certificate is recognised throughout Germany and valued by employers.

Which professions you can learn

There are more than 300 recognised training occupations. Popular fields in Upper Franconia include industrial mechanic, electronics technician, mechatronics, logistics, retail, office management, IT specialist, cook and hotel staff, hairdresser, and many crafts such as plumber, carpenter or painter. Nursing (Pflegefachfrau/Pflegefachmann) and educator training (Erzieherin/Erzieher) are organised partly differently, mainly at specialised schools with practical placements.

Pay during training

Trainees receive a monthly training allowance (Ausbildungsvergütung), which rises each year of training. There is a legal minimum, and many sectors pay more under collective agreements. Trainees are fully covered by health, pension and unemployment insurance. The allowance is modest, so check additional support, such as vocational training assistance, housing benefit or child benefit for trainees under 25.

Is there an age limit?

No. An Ausbildung is open to adults, too. Many people start in their late twenties, thirties or even later, especially when changing careers or when their foreign qualification is not recognised. For adults with work experience there are also shortened training and external exam routes; ask the chamber.

What you need

  • German: most companies expect at least B1, better B2, because vocational school teaches theory in German. Some companies start earlier and combine training with language support.
  • School certificate: legally, no specific certificate is required, but companies select applicants. Have your Ukrainian school certificate translated and, if possible, evaluated.
  • Residence permit: with temporary protection under §24, training is allowed.
  • Motivation: companies value reliability, punctuality and interest in the profession, often more than grades.

Finding the right profession

The career counselling service (Berufsberatung) of the employment agency is free and helps you find professions that suit you, also with aptitude tests. The online database BERUFENET describes every training occupation: tasks, pay, requirements. Internships (Praktikum) of one or two weeks are the best way to test a profession and to get to know a company, and many training contracts start after a good internship.

Timeline and application

Most trainings start on 1 August or 1 September. Large companies select trainees up to a year in advance; smaller companies and crafts often still have places in summer. A German application usually includes a cover letter, a CV in table form and certificates. Many companies in our region also accept a short application by email or a visit in person, especially in crafts.

Tip: the chambers in Upper Franconia publish free training places in their online exchanges, and the employment agency's job portal lists them too. Training fairs (Ausbildungsmessen) in spring and autumn let you talk to companies directly.

Preparing for training: Einstiegsqualifizierung

If you are not yet ready for a full training contract, a long-term paid internship called Einstiegsqualifizierung (EQ) can be a bridge. It lasts six to twelve months in a company, is supported by the employment agency, and often leads directly to a training place in the same company. It is particularly suitable when your German still needs to improve.

Support during training

  • Assisted training (Assistierte Ausbildung): free tutoring, language support and help with problems at school or at work, organised by the employment agency.
  • Vocational training assistance (Berufsausbildungsbeihilfe, BAB): financial support if the allowance is not enough, especially if you live away from your parents.
  • Language courses alongside training, funded by BAMF.
  • Training mentors from volunteer programmes who meet trainees regularly.

Training and Jobcenter benefits

Whether you keep Jobcenter benefits during training depends on your situation, for example your age, whether you live with your family and whether your training can be funded by BAB. In many cases, trainees and their families receive a combination of training allowance, BAB, housing benefit or remaining Jobcenter benefits. Ask the Jobcenter and the employment agency before you sign the contract, so the change goes smoothly.

The training contract

The contract states the profession, duration, probation period (one to four months), weekly hours, holidays and the allowance. Trainees under 18 have extra protection. Before signing, have the contract checked; the chambers check it as well when it is registered. During training, the company must teach you the content of the training regulation and must not use you only for unrelated work.

School-based training: care and education

Some professions are learned mainly at vocational schools with placements. Nursing training is paid and very much in demand; Bavaria needs many care workers. Educator training is longer and partly unpaid, but there are paid variants and funding options. Ask the schools directly about entry requirements, which often include a certain school certificate and German level.

A week in training: what to expect

In the company you work alongside experienced colleagues and a trainer (Ausbilder) who is responsible for you. At first you watch and help, later you take over tasks on your own. You keep a training record (Berichtsheft) in which you note what you learned each week; it is checked before the final exam. At vocational school you study theory, German, economics and social studies. Halfway through, there is an interim exam that shows where you stand.

Common difficulties and solutions

  • Technical German at school: ask for assisted training early and make a word list for your profession.
  • Conflicts at work: talk to your trainer first; the chamber has training advisers (Ausbildungsberater) who mediate free of charge.
  • Wrong profession: switching to another training is possible; talk to the career counselling service before you quit.

After the exam

With the final certificate you are a skilled worker (Fachkraft). Many trainees are offered a permanent job by their training company. Later you can continue with further qualifications such as master craftsman (Meister) or technician, or even study at a university of applied sciences without the Abitur. For your residence, a German qualification and a skilled job are a strong basis for longer-term permits.

Frequently asked questions

Is vocational training paid?

Yes. Trainees receive a monthly training allowance that rises each year, plus full social insurance.

Is there an age limit?

No. Adults can start vocational training at any age.

How good must my German be?

Most companies expect at least B1, better B2. Language support during training is possible.

When does training start?

Usually on 1 August or 1 September. Apply up to a year ahead; crafts often still have places in summer.

What is an Einstiegsqualifizierung?

A paid long-term internship of six to twelve months that prepares for training and often leads to a training place.
